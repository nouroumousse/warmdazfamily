## A landscape is never just a view

The hills, paths and open spaces around Warmdaz are part of how people understand home. A familiar route can hold childhood memories; a place name can preserve knowledge that rarely appears on a map.

Landscape and memory are connected. The way people describe a slope, a field or a water source can reveal how they have lived with the land across generations.

## Listening to place

Documenting local knowledge begins with listening. Residents know details that a photograph alone cannot explain: how a path changes with the seasons, what a place was called, or why a certain spot matters to a family.

That knowledge deserves care. Stories should be shared with permission, names should be checked, and uncertainty should be acknowledged rather than filled with guesses.

## A record for those who come next

Maps, photographs, oral histories and written memories can become a shared record of place. They do not replace lived experience; they give future generations another way to encounter it.

The work starts with curiosity and respect — and with the understanding that a place belongs in the story told by the people who know it best.
